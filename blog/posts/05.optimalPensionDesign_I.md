# Optimal Pension Design: A Naive Approach

In this post we look at why pension plans exist, how they work in Belgium, and how we can naively search for an optimal plan design using a model. The goal is to explain the reasoning behind the model: why you would want such a model in the first place, and how it works under the hood.

## Why a Pension Plan?

The honest answer is that most of us are bad at saving for a future self we have never met. Left to our own devices, we under-save, retire into a consumption cliff, and lean on the state. Combined with the fact that we are living longer than ever, this so-called longevity risk has become a growing concern among professionals. The institutional fix for our tendency to under-invest in the future is the pension plan: a contractual commitment, made while you are earning, to a future version of you. Concretely, your employer sets aside part of your salary in a fund that is invested until you retire.

But there is a second, less obvious answer, and it motivates this whole project: a pension plan is a risk-sharing contract between three parties — employee, employer, and (through guarantees and regulation) the state. The employee wants a stable replacement income; the employer wants predictable, affordable costs; the regulator wants neither party to walk away holding an empty bag. These objectives conflict, and how a plan resolves that conflict is a design choice. Which raises the question this post is really about: can we make that design choice optimally — and what would "optimal" even mean?

## Belgium Legal Framework: the Three pillars 

Belgian retirement income rests on [three pillars](https://www.fsma.be/nl/wat-zijn-de-drie-pensioenpijlers-belgie).

The first pillar is the statutory state pension: a pay-as-you-go system, financed by today's workers for today's retirees, and demographically under pressure in Belgium. For most private-sector careers it replaces only a modest fraction of final salary. For more on the first pillar, I refer you to [Wikifin](https://www.wikifin.be/nl/pensioen-en-pensioenvoorbereiding#paragraph-0).

The second pillar is occupational: pension plans that an employer — or an entire
sector — sets up for its employees, funded by contributions paid in across the
working career. This is where the project lives. The plan is governed by the WAP
(*Wet op de Aanvullende Pensioenen*), and its defining feature is a guaranteed
minimum return on those contributions. That guarantee is what makes the second
pillar more than a savings account: whatever the plan's assets actually earn, the
employee is promised their contributions grow at least at a legally fixed rate.
The risk of falling short doesn't sit with the employee — it sits with the
employer, who *underwrites* the guarantee. If the assets backing the plan return
less than the guaranteed rate, the employer makes up the difference out of pocket.

That guarantee used to be a fixed number — historically 3.75% on employee
contributions and 3.25% on employer contributions. But a fixed guarantee is a
fixed liability, and once safe market yields collapsed well below those levels in
the 2010s, employers were underwriting a spread they had no safe way to earn. The
2016 reform resolved this by letting the guarantee *float* with the market. Since
then it has been a single unified rate, recomputed from the yield on the 10-year
OLO — the Belgian government bond whose curve we simulated in [previous section].
Concretely: 85% of the trailing 24-month average of the 10-year OLO yield, rounded
to the nearest 25 bps, floored at 1.75% and capped at 3.75%, applied separately to
the employer and employee legs:

$$g_t = \operatorname{clip}\!\Big(\operatorname{round}_{25\text{bps}}\big(0.85 \cdot \bar{y}^{\,10Y}_{[t-24m,\,t]}\big),\ 1.75\%,\ 3.75\%\Big).$$

Every term there is a risk-sharing knob. The 85% haircut and the 24-month
averaging make the guarantee lag and dampen the market rather than track it
tick-for-tick; the floor protects the employee when rates are near zero; the cap
protects the employer when they spike.

And this is exactly why the OLO had to come first. Read the formula forward in
time: contributions accrue over a whole career, and the rate applying to each
future year is set off the OLO yield *prevailing in that year*. So the guarantee
the employer is on the hook for isn't a single number — it's a trajectory, a
deterministic function of the entire future path of the 10-year OLO. To know the
distribution of shortfalls the employer might eventually have to cover, we first
need the distribution of future OLO paths. That is precisely what the Hull-White
simulator produces: each simulated short-rate path is mapped back to a future
10-year yield (`reconstructFutureYield`), the formula above is applied to its
trailing average, and out comes the guarantee rate that year's reserve has to
clear. The OLO model isn't scene-setting — it's the engine that turns the WAP
guarantee from a legal definition into a number the environment can compute at
every step.

Sitting alongside the return guarantee are two further hard constraints that
*bound* the plan rather than *price* it: the 80% rule, capping total pension
build-up relative to salary, and the non-discrimination requirements. These enter
the model as feasibility constraints the policy must respect, not as terms in the
objective it trades off.

The third pillar is individual, tax-favoured saving — relevant to the employee's full picture, but outside the scope of this post.

## Different types of Pension Plans 

Within the second pillar the classic dichotomy is:

Defined Benefit (DB): the plan promises an outcome — typically a formula on final or career-average salary — and the employer bears the full investment and longevity risk of delivering it. Increasingly rare, for exactly that reason.

Defined Contribution (DC): the plan promises an input — a contribution rate — and the outcome depends on returns. In most countries this shifts all risk to the employee. Belgium is the interesting exception: the WAP guarantee makes Belgian DC a hybrid, sometimes called "DC with a DB flavour" — contributions are defined, but the employer still stands behind a minimum return.

The insurance vehicle matters too. Branch 21 products credit a contractually guaranteed return (plus discretionary profit sharing) directly to the reserve — the reserve is a credited-return process, not a marked-to-market bond portfolio. Branch 23 products are unit-linked: the reserve tracks fund value, with more upside and no crediting cushion, leaving the WAP guarantee to bite harder in bad scenarios. Comparing how these two vehicles behave under the same contribution policy is one of the axes of the wider project.

This post works with the simplest member of this family: a single-employee Belgian DC plan, Branch 21-style deterministic crediting — for reasons the next section makes clear.

## What is Optimal? 
"Optimal" is meaningless until you say for whom. A contribution policy that thrills the CFO can starve the employee's replacement rate; a generous one can make plan costs procyclical and painful. So the project's central object is a joint value function:

The employer side is the negative of a cost sensitivity index (expected cost, tail risk, procyclicality); the employee side is utility over retirement consumption anchored to a replacement-rate target. The weight λ is not something to optimise — it is the negotiation dial between the two parties, and sweeping it traces out the design frontier.

That is the full framework. This post uses a radically stripped-down version of it, because before optimising anything real, the machinery itself has to be proven trustworthy.

## Reinforcement Learning a Basic Introduction  
For readers whose last contact with RL was an elective: strip the jargon and RL is stochastic control where the strategy is learned rather than fixed at inception.

A state 

s is what you know at a decision point (here: the year, and the accumulated reserve).
An action 

a is a decision (here, brutally simple: contribute or don't, each year).
A policy 

π maps states to actions — a dynamic contribution strategy rather than a fixed schedule.
A value function 

Q(s,a) is the expected sum of future rewards from taking action 

a in state 

s — which, given the reward design above, is literally an expected APV conditional on state and decision. You have computed thousands of these; RL estimates them by simulation and then improves the policy against them.

The method at this rung is tabular Monte Carlo: simulate full episodes under the current policy, record the realised return for every state–action pair visited, and update a table of 

Q-estimates toward those realised returns. No neural network anywhere. The natural objection — "this is solvable by backward induction, why bother with RL?" — is entirely correct, and that is the point: a dynamic-programming-solvable environment is the calibration target. RL earns its keep later, when stochastic credited returns, a funding-ratio state, and a stochastic WAP rate make the transition kernel something you no longer want to enumerate.

Why start this stupid? Because a bug in a complex RL environment is invisible: the agent trains, the reward curve rises, the policy looks plausible — and the objective it optimised is not the one you wrote down. The methodology is a ladder: each rung adds exactly one new element, is validated against an independent benchmark, then frozen as a regression test before the next rung is added.
